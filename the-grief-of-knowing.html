The Grief of Knowing You Could Be Doing More

On wasted time, unrealized potential, and the uncomfortable feeling that you're not becoming the person you thought you'd be.

THE IN-BETWEEN · A PERSONAL ESSAY

There is a particular kind of sadness that comes with being young and already feeling like you're running out of time.

Not time in the literal sense. You know you're young. People remind you constantly. You have your whole life ahead of you, apparently. There are places you haven't seen, people you haven't met, versions of yourself you haven't grown into yet. You're supposed to find comfort in that.

But sometimes, having your whole life ahead of you feels less like a gift and more like a responsibility you're terrified of failing.

Because what if you waste it?

What if you spend your teenage years waiting to become someone worth remembering? What if you spend so much time preparing for your future that you forget to actually live in the present? What if, somewhere between trying to get good grades, make your parents proud, maintain friendships, look good, feel good, and figure out who you're supposed to be, you accidentally become a stranger to yourself?

Nobody really prepares you for the exhaustion of being a person who wants so much out of life.

Especially when you're not entirely sure what that life is supposed to look like.

You want to be successful, but you also want to be happy. You want to be disciplined, but you're tired. You want to make memories, but you're scared of falling behind. You want to enjoy being young, but there's this quiet voice in your head reminding you that every day you're getting older, every year is passing, and every opportunity you don't take is another version of your life you'll never get to experience.

So you make plans.

You promise yourself that tomorrow you'll be better. Tomorrow you'll wake up earlier, study harder, stop procrastinating, stop caring about the wrong things. Tomorrow you'll finally become the person you've been imagining in your head for years.

And sometimes you do.

For a few days, maybe even a few weeks, you feel like you're finally getting somewhere. You're productive. You're motivated. You're proud of yourself in that quiet, private way that makes you feel like maybe, just maybe, you're going to be okay.

Then life happens.

You get tired. You lose focus. You have a bad day that turns into a bad week. You spend an entire evening doing absolutely nothing when you know there were a hundred things you could have done instead.

And suddenly, you're back where you started.

Except now you're disappointed in yourself, because you know what you're capable of. You've seen glimpses of the person you could be, and somehow that makes it harder to accept the person you are right now.

It's a strange thing, knowing you have potential.

People use that word like it's a compliment. You have so much potential. Your teachers say it. Your parents say it. Maybe you say it to yourself when you're trying to feel better about the things you haven't accomplished yet.

But potential can feel like a debt you owe the world.

Every time you rest, you wonder if you should be working. Every time you enjoy yourself, you wonder if you're being irresponsible. Every time someone your age accomplishes something incredible, you congratulate them while quietly calculating all the ways you're falling behind.

You turn other people's milestones into measurements of your own inadequacy.

Someone gets accepted into a program. Someone starts a business. Someone seems to have the perfect group of friends, the perfect grades, the perfect life. You see their achievements and start questioning your own direction.

You forget that you're looking at a single page of their story while judging yourself by every unfinished chapter of yours.

Still, knowing that doesn't always make the feeling go away.

Because sometimes the hardest person to disappoint is the person you thought you would become.

You remember the promises you made to yourself. The goals you wrote down. The person you imagined you'd be by this age. You remember believing that by now, you'd feel more confident, more accomplished, more certain of where you were going.

Instead, you're still figuring things out.

Still second-guessing yourself. Still trying to understand why some days you feel capable of becoming anything and other days you can barely convince yourself that you're doing enough.

And the worst part is that nobody can really see it.

From the outside, you're probably fine. You're going to school. You're laughing at things that are funny. You're replying to messages. You're making plans for the weekend. You might even be doing well.

But inside, there's this constant negotiation between the life you're living and the life you think you should be living.

You feel guilty for being unhappy when nothing is particularly wrong.

You feel guilty for wanting more when you already have things to be grateful for.

You feel guilty for being tired when you haven't even done everything you promised yourself you'd do.

And slowly, without really noticing, you begin treating your life like a problem that needs to be fixed before you can enjoy it.

You tell yourself you'll be happier when your grades improve. When you become more confident. When you find better friends. When you finally get your life together.

You keep moving the finish line.

You convince yourself that happiness is waiting somewhere in the future, just beyond the next achievement, the next improvement, the next version of yourself.

But what happens when you get there?

What happens when you accomplish the thing you spent months worrying about, and instead of feeling fulfilled, you immediately start worrying about the next thing?

What happens when you finally become the person you wanted to be, only to discover that you've spent so much time trying to get there that you don't know how to appreciate being her?

I think that's what scares me most about growing up.

Not that I'll fail. Not even that I'll make the wrong decisions.

It's that I'll spend so much of my life trying to become someone that I'll forget I was someone all along.

That I'll look back at my teenage years and remember them primarily through the things I was anxious about. The grades I wanted. The people I wanted to impress. The opportunities I was scared of missing. The weekends I spent feeling guilty about not being productive enough.

That I'll remember being seventeen and realize I was so busy worrying about whether my life was going anywhere that I forgot to notice where it already was.

The ordinary things, especially.

The conversations that lasted longer than they needed to. The laughter that made your stomach hurt. The songs you played so often they became attached to an entire season of your life. The walk home when the sky looked beautiful for no particular reason. The friendships that felt permanent because you couldn't imagine a world in which they weren't.

The versions of yourself you were before you learned to criticize every little thing.

Maybe you didn't appreciate those moments because they didn't feel important enough to appreciate.

Maybe you thought life would begin when something extraordinary happened.

But life was happening.

It was happening in the middle of your ordinary Tuesday, while you were worrying about your future. It was happening in the bedroom you wanted to redecorate, the school year you couldn't wait to finish, the conversations you barely paid attention to because your mind was somewhere else.

You were living days you would someday miss without knowing they were worth missing.

And I wonder how many beautiful things we've overlooked simply because we were too busy trying to become better versions of ourselves to love the versions we already were.

This isn't to say that ambition is bad. It isn't wrong to want more from yourself. It isn't wrong to have goals, to work hard, to recognize that you have things you want to change.

There is something beautiful about wanting to grow.

But I don't think growth is supposed to feel like constantly apologizing for who you are.

I don't think becoming better should require you to hate who you are now.

And I don't think you need to earn the right to enjoy your own life.

You can be ambitious and still take the afternoon off. You can want to improve your grades without letting a disappointing mark convince you that you're disappointing as a person. You can recognize your flaws without turning them into reasons to dislike yourself.

You can be a work in progress without treating yourself like a failed project.

Because you are not behind simply because you haven't figured everything out.

You're not wasting your life every time you have a day without a purpose. You're not falling behind because someone else discovered their passion before you did. You're not failing because your progress looks different from what you imagined.

And maybe you will make mistakes. Maybe there will be opportunities you miss, friendships you outgrow, decisions you wish you could take back. Maybe there will be years when you feel lost in ways you cannot explain to anyone else.

That doesn't mean you've ruined anything.

It means you're living a life that you couldn't possibly know how to navigate perfectly.

You're learning as you go. You're becoming someone in real time, without a finished version of yourself to use as a guide.

Perhaps that's the part we don't talk about enough: you are allowed to be unfinished while your life is happening.

You don't have to wait until you're confident to make memories. You don't have to wait until you're successful to feel proud of yourself. You don't have to wait until you've healed every insecurity, answered every question, or figured out your entire future before you let yourself be happy.

You can begin where you are.

Not where you thought you would be by now. Not where someone else your age seems to be. Not where you imagine you'll finally be worthy of your own approval.

Here.

In the middle of the uncertainty. In the middle of the growing pains. In the middle of a life that is neither everything you dreamed of nor anything close to being over.

And maybe the goal isn't to stop wanting more.

Maybe it's to stop making the life you have feel like it's worth less because of it.

Because one day, you'll probably look back at the person you are right now with a tenderness you don't yet know how to give yourself.

You'll see someone who was trying so hard. Someone who cared deeply about getting things right. Someone who was frightened of wasting time because, somewhere inside, they understood how precious it was.

And I hope you don't spend that day wishing you had been kinder to yourself.

I hope you remember that you tried, even when trying looked like starting over for the hundredth time. That you kept going, even when you had no idea what you were doing. That you laughed, loved, made mistakes, changed your mind, and became someone a little different with every passing year.

I hope you understand that your life was never just the sum of the things you achieved.

It was also the things you felt. The people you loved. The courage it took to try something new. The quiet decisions nobody applauded. The days you survived without making anything extraordinary out of them.

You didn't have to turn every moment into something meaningful for your life to mean something.

And maybe, if there's anything worth remembering as you grow older, it's this:

You don't need to become the best possible version of yourself before you deserve to be present for your own life.

You are not a deadline.

You are not a list of goals waiting to be completed.

You are not a future version of yourself who has yet to arrive.

You are a person, right now, in the middle of becoming.

And I hope you give yourself the chance to experience that.

Not just to look back on it someday.

But to actually be here for it.

For the girl who keeps promising herself she'll start living once she finally gets her life together.
